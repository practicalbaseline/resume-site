Where "Tokens" Came From: From Asimov to Attention Mechanisms

10 min read

When engineers talk about "tokens" in the context of large language models, they mean discrete subword units that models process sequentially. But the word token carries centuries of meaning — as a physical object exchanged for value, as a symbol standing in for something else, as a unit of access or permission. Science fiction picked up this lineage long before neural networks existed, and the conceptual DNA shows up in how we design AI systems today.

The original meaning

A token, in its oldest sense, is a tangible object that represents something intangible. Roman soldiers carried tesserae — small clay or bone markers — as proof of identity or entitlement. Medieval merchants used trade tokens when official coinage ran short. In every case, the token wasn't the thing itself; it was a stand-in that granted access, proved membership, or held value by agreement.

This is precisely what a token is in NLP: a stand-in for a fragment of meaning. The word "understanding" might become ["under", "stand", "ing"] — three tokens representing one concept. The model never sees the word; it sees the tokens.

Tokens in Asimov's universe

Isaac Asimov never used the word "token" in the modern ML sense — the field didn't exist in his lifetime. But his work is saturated with the concept of discrete symbolic units that carry meaning, grant access, or encode rules.

The Three Laws as tokens of ethics

Asimov's Three Laws of Robotics are, in a sense, the most famous tokens in science fiction. Sixty words — a fixed, discrete set of rules hardwired into every positronic brain. They don't describe intelligence; they constrain it. Each law is a token of permission and prohibition, parsed in order, with conflicts resolved by priority.

A robot may not injure a human being or, through inaction, allow a human being to come to harm.

Sound familiar? Modern system prompts work the same way: a fixed set of rules prepended to every request, constraining the model's behavior before any user input is processed. Asimov wrote the first system prompt in 1942.

Multivac and the insufficient data problem

In The Last Question (1956), humanity feeds successive generations of supercomputers — Multivac, Microvac, Galactic AC, Cosmic AC — a single query about reversing entropy. The answer, repeated across eons:

INSUFFICIENT DATA FOR MEANINGFUL ANSWER.

This is a token budget problem avant la lettre. Each generation of the computer has more data, more capacity, more context — but the question demands more than any finite context window can hold. The story's resolution (spoiler: the computer becomes the universe and says "Let there be light") is Asimov's answer to what happens when you finally have enough tokens.

Symbolic access in the Foundation series

In Foundation, the Encyclopedia Galactica serves as a token of knowledge — a symbolic repository that grants its possessors the ability to shape civilization. The Prime Radiant, the visual tool used by Second Foundation psychohistorians, encodes the future as discrete, manipulable symbols. Hari Seldon's messages are tokens of prophecy: fixed strings that unlock when the right conditions are met.

These aren't tokens in the BPE sense, but they embody the same principle: compress complex reality into discrete units that can be stored, transmitted, and acted upon.

Science fiction's broader token vocabulary

Access tokens and identity

Cyberpunk fiction made the security token literal. In William Gibson's Neuromancer (1984), access to cyberspace requires ICE breakers, passwords, and neural jacks — physical-digital tokens that grant or deny entry. The concept of a bearer token (possession grants access, no questions asked) pervades the genre.

Today's API keys and JWT bearer tokens follow the same logic Gibson imagined: a compact credential that stands in for a larger identity, passed with every request.

Currency tokens

Science fiction has long explored economies built on tokens rather than traditional money. In many dystopian futures, citizens receive ration tokens for food, water, or information access. These stories explore what happens when the token of value becomes disconnected from the thing it represents — a question that maps directly onto discussions of cryptocurrency, API credits, and compute budgets.

Memory tokens in AI fiction

Stories about artificial minds frequently treat memories as discrete, addressable units. In Philip K. Dick's work, identity itself is a token that can be copied, forged, or revoked. The replicants in Blade Runner carry implanted memory tokens — fabricated experiences encoded as discrete narrative units.

Modern LLMs with retrieval-augmented generation do something analogous: memories (documents, facts, prior conversations) are chunked into retrievable tokens and injected into the context window when relevant.

From fiction to formalism

The bridge from literary tokens to computational tokens happened in linguistics and information theory, not in sci-fi:

  1. 1950s–60s: Information theory (Shannon) treats messages as sequences of discrete symbols drawn from a finite alphabet — the mathematical birth of "token" as a unit of information.
  2. 1990s: Statistical NLP tokenizes text into words and punctuation for parsing and machine translation.
  3. 2016: Google's SentencePiece and OpenAI's BPE (Byte Pair Encoding) popularize subword tokenization — splitting text into variable-length fragments that balance vocabulary size with coverage.
  4. 2017: The Transformer architecture ("Attention Is All You Need") processes sequences of these tokens in parallel, with self-attention determining which tokens matter to which other tokens.
  5. 2020s: "Token" becomes the billing unit, the context window unit, and the fundamental currency of the LLM economy.

Why the metaphor matters

Understanding that "token" isn't an arbitrary engineering term — but a concept with deep roots in symbolism, access control, and information theory — changes how you design systems:

  • Tokens are stand-ins, not the thing itself. The model processes tokens, not meaning. Never confuse the representation with the reality.
  • Tokens grant access. Your context window is a finite resource. Every token you include is a token of permission — this information is important enough to consume budget.
  • Tokens encode rules. System prompts are Asimov's Three Laws: fixed constraints prepended to every interaction.
  • Insufficient data is a real answer. Sometimes the right response is to say you don't have enough context, not to hallucinate the rest.

Asimov imagined robots governed by sixty words of embedded ethics. We build language models governed by system prompts, context windows, and token budgets. The vocabulary changed; the problem didn't. How do you constrain intelligence with discrete rules? How do you allocate finite symbolic resources? How do you know when you have insufficient data for a meaningful answer?

Science fiction asked these questions decades before the technology existed to make them urgent. We're living in the answer now.
